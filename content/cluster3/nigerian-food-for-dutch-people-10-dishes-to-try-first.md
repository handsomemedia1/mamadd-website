# Nigerian Food for Dutch People: 10 Dishes to Try First

If you grew up in the Netherlands, your culinary baseline is likely built on comforting, practical, and hearty foods. You probably love a good *stamppot* on a cold winter evening, enjoy a quick *broodje kaas* for lunch, and look forward to the deep-fried comfort of *bitterballen* when enjoying a *biertje* with friends. Traditional Dutch food is mild, warming, and heavily reliant on potatoes, vegetables, and straightforward meats.

Nigerian cuisine, on the other hand, is a vibrant explosion of color, spice, smoke, and complex flavors. It is bold, unapologetic, and highly communal. For a Dutch person exploring West African food for the first time, the transition can be overwhelming. The textures are different, the spices are much hotter, and the concept of eating a "swallow" with your hands instead of using a knife and fork can feel entirely foreign.

But do not let that intimidate you! Nigerian food is incredibly comforting once you understand the flavor profiles. It shares the Dutch appreciation for hearty, filling meals that bring people together. If you are in Enschede and curious about expanding your culinary horizons, Mama DD’s African Kitchen is the perfect place to start.

To help you navigate the menu, we have compiled a beginner-friendly guide. Here are 10 Nigerian dishes to try first, explained in a way that makes sense to a Dutch palate.

## 1. Puff-Puff (The Nigerian Oliebol)

Let’s start with something familiar. If you love *oliebollen* during the festive season, you will absolutely adore Puff-Puff. 

Puff-Puff is a popular Nigerian street food and party appetizer (part of what Nigerians call "small chops"). Like oliebollen, Puff-Puff consists of a sweet yeast dough that is deep-fried until golden brown. However, Puff-Puff is simpler: it doesn't contain raisins or apple bits, and it is rarely dusted with powdered sugar. The result is a soft, spongy, airy ball of dough with a subtle, comforting sweetness. It is the perfect, safe gateway into Nigerian food and is beloved by children and adults alike.

## 2. Jollof Rice (The Ultimate Comfort Food)

If you only try one Nigerian dish in your life, make it Jollof Rice. 

Imagine a much richer, spicier, and smokier version of Spanish paella or a highly seasoned risotto, but without the seafood. Jollof rice is made by cooking parboiled long-grain rice in a robust, deeply flavorful broth of blended tomatoes, red bell peppers, onions, and scotch bonnet peppers. The signature of Nigerian Jollof is its "smoky" flavor, achieved by intentionally charring the rice at the bottom of the pot. It is savory, slightly spicy, and incredibly hearty. Think of it as the ultimate comfort food—the Nigerian equivalent of a really good, slow-cooked *hachee*, but replacing the potatoes with vibrant, spicy rice.

## 3. Suya (The West African Saté)

Are you a fan of *kipsaté* or a good barbecue? Then Suya is for you. 

Suya is the quintessential Nigerian street food. It consists of thinly sliced meat (usually beef, chicken, or goat) that is marinated in a complex dry rub called *Yaji*. This spice mix is made from ground peanuts, ginger, garlic, cayenne pepper, and bouillon. The skewered meat is then grilled over an open flame, giving it a smoky, charred exterior. The peanut base of the spice mix provides a nutty, savory flavor profile that will remind you of a highly spiced, dry version of Indonesian satay sauce. It is usually served with raw red onions and is the perfect accompaniment to a cold beer.

## 4. Dodo (Fried Sweet Plantains)

You cannot have a Nigerian meal without a side of Dodo. 

Plantains look like large bananas, but they are starchier and are meant to be cooked before eating. To make Dodo, ripe (yellow or black) plantains are sliced and deep-fried until they are golden brown and caramelized around the edges. They are soft, sweet, and slightly crispy. The sweetness of the Dodo acts as a perfect counter-balance to the intense heat and savory spices of dishes like Jollof rice. If you have a bit of a sweet tooth but still want a savory meal, Dodo is the perfect bridge.

## 5. Meat Pie (The Hearty Snack)

If you enjoy a good *saucijzenbroodje* (sausage roll) or a savory pastry from the local Dutch bakery, you will easily fall in love with the Nigerian Meat Pie. 

Unlike the flaky, puff pastry used in Dutch sausage rolls, the pastry for a Nigerian Meat Pie is rich, dense, and slightly sweet, similar to shortcrust. The inside is filled with a savory, heavily seasoned mixture of minced meat, potatoes, and carrots. It is a hearty, filling snack that is perfect for lunch on the go. It is deeply comforting and completely devoid of the intense chili heat found in other Nigerian dishes, making it very accessible for a sensitive palate.

## 6. Pounded Yam and Egusi Soup (The Classic "Swallow")

Now we enter the heart of traditional Nigerian dining: the "swallows" and soups. This concept might be the most foreign to a Dutch diner. Instead of using cutlery, you eat these dishes with your hands, using a starchy dough (the swallow) to scoop up the soup.

Think of the swallow as acting like the mashed potatoes in a *stamppot*, providing a neutral, filling base for a flavorful topping. 

**Pounded Yam** is the most premium swallow. It is made by boiling white yams and pounding them until they form a smooth, stretchy, and soft dough. It has a very mild, slightly sweet flavor.

**Egusi Soup** is the perfect pairing for a beginner. It is a thick, rich soup made from ground melon seeds (Egusi), palm oil, leafy greens (like spinach), and assorted meats. The ground seeds give the soup a nutty, curd-like texture, somewhat reminiscent of scrambled eggs but entirely savory and deeply flavored with meat stock and dried fish. Taking a piece of Pounded Yam, dipping it into the rich Egusi soup, and eating it is an essential Nigerian culinary experience.

## 7. Fried Rice (The Mild Alternative)

If the spicy, smoky flavor of Jollof rice sounds a bit too intimidating, Nigerian Fried Rice is an excellent alternative. 

Do not confuse it with the *nasi goreng* you get from the local Dutch-Indonesian takeaway. Nigerian Fried Rice does not use soy sauce or *ketjap manis*. Instead, parboiled rice is cooked in rich meat stock and curry powder, which gives it a beautiful yellow-green color. It is then stir-fried with an abundance of vegetables (carrots, green beans, sweet corn, peas) and small pieces of fried liver or shrimp. It is savory, aromatic, slightly sweet from the vegetables, and generally much milder in spice than Jollof. 

## 8. Asun (For the Brave Spice Lovers)

If you consider yourself a spice enthusiast—if you always ask for extra sambal with your meals—then you must try Asun. 

Asun consists of bite-sized pieces of roasted or smoked goat meat that are aggressively tossed in a chunky, fiery sauce made from scotch bonnet peppers and onions. It is intensely spicy, smoky, and chewy. Goat meat has a distinct, robust flavor that stands up well to the heavy spices. This is typically eaten as a snack or appetizer and is meant to make you sweat. It is the ultimate bar food.

## 9. Ayamase / Designer Stew (The Flavor Bomb)

If you are feeling adventurous and want to experience the full depth of Nigerian umami, try Ayamase (often called Designer Stew). 

This is a unique, dark stew made from bleached palm oil, green bell peppers, and fermented locust beans (called *Iru*). The locust beans give the stew an incredibly pungent, earthy, and deep umami flavor—somewhat similar to the funkiness of a strong French cheese or Asian fish sauce, but uniquely West African. It is loaded with assorted meats and boiled eggs, and is traditionally served over Ofada rice (a local, robust short-grain rice) or standard white rice. It is spicy, rich, and unforgettable.

## 10. Pepper Soup (The Ultimate Cure-All)

In the Netherlands, when you are feeling sick or simply need warming up on a cold, rainy day, you reach for a bowl of *erwtensoep* (pea soup) or chicken broth. In Nigeria, you reach for Pepper Soup.

Unlike thick European soups, Nigerian Pepper Soup is a light, watery broth. However, it packs a massive punch. It is flavored with a specific blend of indigenous West African spices (like calabash nutmeg and grains of selim) that give it a highly aromatic, medicinal, and deeply warming quality. It is also quite spicy (hence the name) and usually contains cuts of goat meat, fresh fish, or chicken. It clears the sinuses, warms the body from the inside out, and is incredibly comforting.

## A Quick Note on Dining Etiquette: Eating with Your Hands

When you order a "swallow" dish like Pounded Yam and Egusi soup, you might be surprised when you are not automatically handed a knife and fork. In Nigerian culture, swallows are traditionally eaten with the right hand. 

This tactile approach to eating is not about a lack of manners; rather, it is an essential part of the culinary experience. Feeling the temperature and texture of the food prepares your palate, and many Nigerians believe the food genuinely tastes better when eaten this way. 

To eat like a local, pinch off a small piece of the dough with your right thumb and first two fingers. Roll it into a smooth ball, make a small indentation in the center with your thumb, and use it as a scoop to pick up a generous amount of the soup and meat. It can be messy at first, but it is deeply satisfying. Of course, if you are uncomfortable, do not hesitate to ask for cutlery—the goal is to enjoy the food, however you choose to eat it!
