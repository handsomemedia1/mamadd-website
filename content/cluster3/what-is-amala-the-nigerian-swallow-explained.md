# What Is Amala? The Nigerian Swallow Explained

If you have ever visited a traditional Nigerian restaurant, or browsed the menu of an African eatery like Mama DD’s in Enschede, you might have seen a dark, dough-like side dish served alongside vibrant, rich soups. This unique and beloved dish is called *àmàlà* (or simply amala). For those new to West African cuisine, amala can look unusual and unfamiliar. Yet, for millions of Nigerians—especially the Yoruba people of southwestern Nigeria—amala is the ultimate comfort food. 

So, what exactly is amala? Amala is a popular Nigerian "swallow" made from dried, grated, and roasted yam skins or cassava flour. It is traditionally served piping hot alongside rich, flavorful Nigerian soups like Ewedu, Gbegiri, or Egusi, and is meant to be pinched with the fingers, dipped into the soup, and swallowed without chewing. 

Let’s explore the origins, variations, and cultural significance of amala, and dive into how you can enjoy this authentic Nigerian staple.

## The Concept of "Swallow" in Nigerian Cuisine

To understand amala, you first need to understand the concept of "swallow" foods. In many Western cultures, a meal is centered around a piece of meat with side dishes like potatoes, rice, or pasta. In the Netherlands, for example, *stamppot* acts as a hearty, starchy base for sausage or bacon. 

In Nigeria, meals are often centered around a flavorful, highly seasoned soup or stew. To eat this soup, you need a starchy, dough-like accompaniment that acts as an edible utensil. These starchy sides are collectively called "swallows." 

Swallows are made by boiling root vegetables (like yams or cassava) or grains, and pounding or stirring them until they form a smooth, thick, elastic dough. Instead of using a spoon or fork, you wash your hands, pinch off a small piece of the swallow, roll it into a ball, make a small indentation with your thumb, and use it to scoop up the soup. You then swallow the combination—hence the name!

Amala is one of the most famous and culturally significant types of swallow. 

## What Is Amala Made From?

Unlike other swallows such as Pounded Yam (which is white and made from fresh yams) or Eba (which is yellow or white and made from fermented cassava), amala is distinctively dark. Its color ranges from light brown to deep, almost black-brown, depending on how it is prepared and the specific ingredients used. 

There are three main types of amala, each made from a different base ingredient:

### 1. Amala Isu (Yam Flour Amala)
This is the most common and popular type of amala. It is made from yam flour, known as *elubo*. To make elubo, fresh yams are peeled, sliced, cleaned, dried in the sun, and then ground into a fine powder. During the drying process, the yams turn brown. When this brown flour is mixed with boiling water and stirred vigorously over heat, it becomes the smooth, dark dough known as Amala Isu.

### 2. Amala Lafun (Cassava Flour Amala)
This variation is made from cassava flour. The cassava is peeled, soaked in water to ferment, dried, and ground into flour. Amala Lafun is lighter in color than Amala Isu, often appearing pale grey or off-white. It has a slightly different texture and a faintly sour taste due to the fermentation of the cassava.

### 3. Amala Ogede (Plantain Flour Amala)
Made from unripe plantains that are dried and ground into flour, Amala Ogede is a healthier alternative for people looking to reduce their carbohydrate intake or manage their blood sugar. It is lighter in color, similar to Amala Lafun, but has a subtle, earthy flavor profile unique to plantains.

## What Does Amala Taste Like?

If you are trying amala for the first time, you might wonder what to expect in terms of flavor and texture. 

On its own, amala has a very mild, earthy, and slightly roasted flavor. The yam flour version has a distinct, comforting earthiness that pairs beautifully with bold flavors. The texture is soft, light, and elastic—lighter than Pounded Yam, making it less heavy on the stomach. 

However, amala is never eaten on its own. Its true magic is revealed when it is paired with Nigerian soups. The swallow itself acts as a neutral, comforting base that soaks up the intense spices, umami flavors, and savory notes of the accompanying dish.

## How Is Amala Served? The Classic "Abula" Combo

While amala can be eaten with almost any Nigerian soup, there is a legendary, classic combination that is considered the ultimate way to enjoy it. This combination is known as *Abula*.

Abula is a triumvirate of three distinct components served together with the amala:

1. **Ewedu Soup**: A green, slightly viscous soup made from jute leaves. It is mild, savory, and provides a smooth texture that makes the amala glide down easily.
2. **Gbegiri Soup**: A rich, yellowish-orange soup made from blended black-eyed peas, palm oil, and spices. It adds a hearty, earthy, and slightly sweet flavor to the mix.
3. **Obe Ata (Buka Stew)**: A vibrant, spicy red stew made from red bell peppers, tomatoes, onions, and scotch bonnet peppers. It contains assorted meats (like beef, tripe, and cow foot) and provides the heat and umami that tie the dish together.

When these three soups are poured over a hot mound of amala, the result is a symphony of flavors, textures, and colors. The savory meat stew, the creamy beans, the earthy jute leaves, and the soft yam dough create an unforgettable culinary experience.

## The Cultural Significance of Amala

Amala is more than just food; it is a cultural institution, particularly among the Yoruba people. In southwestern Nigeria, especially in cities like Ibadan (often considered the capital of amala), this dish is a staple of everyday life, celebrations, and gatherings. 

It is commonly sold in local eateries known as *Bukas* or *Mama Put* joints. In these bustling, informal restaurants, large pots of boiling soups and steaming amala are served to hungry patrons from all walks of life. Eating amala at a Buka is an experience characterized by warmth, community, and the shared love of authentic, unpretentious food.

In recent years, amala has transcended its regional origins and has become a beloved dish across Nigeria and the global diaspora. It represents heritage, tradition, and the soul of Yoruba cuisine.

## How to Eat Amala (A Guide for Beginners)

If you are a Dutch local in Enschede or someone new to West African food, eating amala for the first time might seem intimidating. Here is a simple step-by-step guide to enjoying it the traditional way:

1. **Wash Your Hands**: Amala is a finger food. Before the meal begins, you will usually be provided with a bowl of warm water and soap to wash your hands. Always use your right hand to eat, as using the left hand is considered impolite in Nigerian culture.
2. **Pinch and Roll**: Pinch off a bite-sized piece of the amala dough using your fingers. Roll it gently into a small ball.
3. **Make a Scoop**: Use your thumb to create a small indentation in the ball of dough. This turns the amala into a makeshift spoon.
4. **Dip and Scoop**: Dip the amala into the soup, making sure to scoop up some of the rich stew, Ewedu, or Gbegiri. 
5. **Swallow**: Place the amala and soup in your mouth. Traditionally, you do not chew the swallow; you simply swallow it, allowing the flavors of the soup to coat your palate.
6. **Enjoy the Meat**: The pieces of meat or fish in the stew can be eaten separately, chewing and savoring the tender, spicy protein.
