# 10 Nigerian Foods to Try If You've Never Had Nigerian Food Before

If you are new to West African cuisine, walking into a Nigerian restaurant or looking at a menu can feel like stepping into a completely different world. The flavors are bold, the ingredients are unique, and the eating etiquette—like eating dense starchy doughs with your hands—might be entirely unfamiliar. 

But do not let the unfamiliarity intimidate you! Nigerian food is incredibly rich, comforting, and deeply satisfying. If you are ready to expand your culinary horizons, here are the 10 best Nigerian foods for beginners to try.

**What is the best Nigerian food for beginners?**  
For complete beginners, the best Nigerian foods to try are Jollof Rice (a flavorful, smoky, tomato-based rice dish) served with fried plantains, Suya (spicy, peanut-rubbed grilled beef skewers), and Egusi soup paired with Pounded Yam (a mild, dough-like side dish). These offer a gentle, delicious introduction to the bold spices and rich flavors of West Africa.

---

## 1. Jollof Rice (The Undisputed Gateway)

If you only try one Nigerian dish in your life, it must be Jollof rice. It is the king of West African cuisine, a staple at every party, and a source of intense national pride. 

Jollof is a vibrant, one-pot dish where long-grain rice is cooked in a rich, heavily spiced broth made from blended tomatoes, red bell peppers, onions, scotch bonnets, and meat stock. Authentic Nigerian "Party Jollof" has a distinct, irresistible smoky flavor. It is spicy, deeply savory, and universally loved. 

*Beginner Tip: Always order it with a side of fried plantains (dodo) and a piece of fried or grilled chicken.*

## 2. Fried Plantains (Dodo)

While technically a side dish, *dodo* is essential to the Nigerian eating experience. Sweet, ripe plantains are sliced and deep-fried until their edges are caramelized and slightly crispy, while the inside remains soft and sweet. 

The natural sweetness of the fried plantains provides the perfect counter-balance to the intense, fiery heat of dishes like Jollof rice or spicy red stews. 

## 3. Suya (Spicy Grilled Meat Skewers)

Suya is the undisputed champion of Nigerian street food, originally hailing from the Hausa people of northern Nigeria. 

It consists of thinly sliced beef, chicken, or ram that is heavily coated in *yaji*—a complex, dry spice rub made from ground roasted peanuts, ginger, garlic, cayenne pepper, and bouillon. The skewers are then grilled over an open charcoal flame. The result is a smoky, nutty, incredibly savory, and spicy piece of meat that is totally addictive. It is usually served with raw red onions and tomatoes to cut the heat.

## 4. Egusi Soup with Pounded Yam

If you are ready to venture into traditional Nigerian soups—which are thick, hearty, and eaten with a starchy dough called a "swallow"—Egusi is the perfect starting point.

The soup is made from ground melon seeds (egusi), which form soft, savory curds when cooked in red palm oil and meat stock. It is packed with leafy greens (like spinach or bitter leaf) and assorted meats. The flavor is nutty, rich, and earthy.

*Beginner Tip: Pair it with Pounded Yam. Pounded Yam is a smooth, slightly sweet, pillowy dough that is very mild, making it the perfect vehicle for the rich Egusi soup. Remember, you eat this with your hands!*

## 5. Beef or Chicken Stew (Obe Ata)

If the idea of eating soup with your hands is still a bit too intimidating, Red Stew is an excellent alternative. 

*Obe Ata* is a vibrant, bright red stew made by blending red bell peppers, tomatoes, onions, and scotch bonnets, and frying the mixture in oil until it becomes thick and intensely flavorful. It is usually packed with pieces of tender beef or chicken. It is spicy, slightly tart, and very savory. You simply eat it with a spoon over a plate of plain boiled white rice.

## 6. Moi Moi (Steamed Bean Pudding)

Moi Moi is a savory, steamed bean pudding that is completely unique to West African cuisine. 

Black-eyed peas are soaked, peeled, and blended with onions, red peppers, and spices into a smooth batter. The batter is often enriched with hard-boiled eggs, bits of fish, or corned beef, then poured into banana leaves (or small containers) and steamed until firm. It has a soft, spongey, melt-in-your-mouth texture and a deeply savory flavor. It is often eaten as a side dish alongside Jollof rice.

## 7. Meat Pies

Nigerian meat pies are a beloved snack and the ultimate comfort food. They are similar to British Cornish pasties or Jamaican beef patties but have their own distinct character.

The pastry is incredibly rich, buttery, and flaky. Inside, it is stuffed with a savory, heavily seasoned filling of minced beef, diced potatoes, and carrots. They are mild in spice but huge in flavor, making them a very safe and delicious option for absolute beginners.

## 8. Efo Riro (Rich Spinach Stew)

If you love vegetables, Efo Riro will blow your mind. Originating from the Yoruba people of southwestern Nigeria, this is not a boring, watery side salad. 

Efo Riro is a rich, heavy stew where massive amounts of leafy greens (like spinach) are cooked in red palm oil, blended peppers, fermented locust beans (*iru*), ground crayfish, and an abundance of assorted meats and smoked fish. It is earthy, deeply umami, spicy, and incredibly robust. It is best eaten with a swallow like Pounded Yam or Eba.

## 9. Puff-Puff (Sweet Fried Dough)

Every cuisine needs a sweet treat, and in Nigeria, that treat is Puff-Puff. 

These are simple, deep-fried balls of yeast dough. When fried correctly, they are golden and slightly crispy on the outside, and incredibly soft, airy, and chewy on the inside. They are mildly sweet, often carrying a faint hint of nutmeg. They are served warm and are dangerously easy to eat by the dozen.

## 10. Asun (Spicy Roasted Goat Meat)

If you have tried Suya and realize you love Nigerian heat and meat, Asun is your next step. 

Asun is a spicy, roasted goat meat dish. The goat meat (complete with the skin, which adds a wonderful chewy texture) is roasted and then chopped into bite-sized pieces. It is then tossed in a fiery, chunky mixture of sautéed onions, green and red scotch bonnet peppers. It is incredibly spicy, smoky, and bursting with the distinct, rich flavor of goat meat.


## Nigerian Drinks to Pair with Your Meal

To truly experience a Nigerian meal like a local, you need the right beverage. Water is fine, but Nigerian food, with its intense heat, heavy starches, and rich flavors, demands something with a bit more character to wash it down and balance the palate.

- **Maltina or Malta Guinness:** These are non-alcoholic malt drinks that are ubiquitous in Nigeria and the diaspora. They are dark, heavily carbonated, and intensely sweet and syrupy. The thick sweetness is the absolute perfect remedy when your mouth is burning from a spicy spoonful of Jollof rice or Suya. 
- **Zobo (Hibiscus Tea):** A vibrant, ruby-red drink made from boiling dried hibiscus flowers with ginger, cloves, and sometimes pineapple peels. It is served ice cold. The tartness of the hibiscus and the sharp, spicy kick of the ginger make it an incredibly refreshing palate cleanser, especially after eating a heavy, oily dish like Egusi soup.
- **Chapman:** If you are dining at a restaurant or attending a Nigerian party, you must order a Chapman. It is Nigeria's most famous mocktail—a bright red, fruity mix of orange soda (Fanta), lemon-lime soda (Sprite), blackcurrant cordial (Ribena), and a dash of Angostura bitters, garnished with cucumber and citrus slices. It is sweet, complex, and incredibly refreshing.
- **Palm Wine:** For the adventurous, sweet palm wine (a natural, mildly alcoholic sap tapped from palm trees) offers an authentic, traditional pairing for heavy soups and swallows, providing a slightly sour, fizzy contrast.

## Tips for Your First Nigerian Restaurant Visit

Stepping into a Nigerian restaurant for the first time should be an exciting cultural experience, not an intimidating one. Here are a few tips to help you navigate the experience like a pro:

1. **Embrace Eating with Your Hands:** If you order a soup like Egusi or Efo Riro with a swallow (like Pounded Yam or Eba), do not ask for a spoon! Wash your hands (restaurants will always provide a sink or bring a bowl of warm water and soap to your table) and dig in. Remember to only use your right hand to pinch the dough, scoop the soup, and eat. It connects you to the food in a completely new way.
2. **Ask About the Heat Level:** Authentic Nigerian food is cooked with scotch bonnet and habanero peppers, which are significantly hotter than jalapeños. If you have a low tolerance for spice, ask the server if the dish can be made milder. However, dishes like Jollof rice or Red Stew are often pre-cooked in large batches, so the heat level might be fixed. 
3. **Be Prepared to Wait:** Good Nigerian food takes time. Unlike fast food, dishes are often finished to order, and yams might be pounded fresh. Enjoy a cold Malt drink, chat with your companions, and embrace the relaxed, communal atmosphere. The food is absolutely worth the wait.
4. **Don't Be Afraid of 'Assorted Meats':** If you see 'assorted meats' on the menu, it usually means a mix of beef, tripe, and cow skin. It is meant to provide a variety of textures (soft, chewy, gelatinous). If you prefer just beef or chicken, you can usually request that instead!
\n\n## The Dessert Question: Where Are the Sweets?

If you have a sweet tooth, you might notice that traditional Nigerian restaurant menus don't have a large dessert section. Unlike Western cuisines that emphasize heavy cakes, pastries, or ice creams after a meal, Nigerian cuisine typically does not focus on post-dinner desserts. The meals themselves are so heavy, starchy, and filling that adding a rich dessert is usually unnecessary. 

However, when Nigerians do want something sweet after a meal, it is usually very simple:
- **Fresh Fruit:** A platter of perfectly ripe, sweet watermelon, pineapple, and pawpaw (papaya) is the most common way to cleanse the palate.
- **Puff-Puff and Meat Pies:** While technically appetizers or snacks, these sweet and savory pastries are sometimes enjoyed at the end of a meal with a cold drink.
- **Chin-Chin:** Small, crunchy, deep-fried pieces of sweet dough, often flavored with nutmeg. These are usually eaten as a snack throughout the day but make a great sweet finish.
